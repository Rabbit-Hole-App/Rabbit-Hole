Let's walk through how a decision tree decides. Imagine you want to predict whether a customer will buy a product. The tree starts at the top with one question about your data, for example, is the customer older than thirty? Everyone answering yes goes down the right branch, and everyone answering no goes down the left branch. Each branch then asks its own next question, and the questions keep splitting the group into smaller and smaller groups. The key idea is how the tree picks which question to ask. At every step it tries many possible questions and measures how mixed each resulting group is. A group where almost everyone bought is a clean group, and a group that is half buyers and half non buyers is a messy one. The tree chooses the question that makes the groups as clean as possible. Two common measures of messiness are called Gini impurity and entropy, and both give a low score when a group is mostly one answer. The tree repeats this choice again and again until a group is pure, or until it hits a limit, such as a maximum depth. At the very bottom are the leaves, and each leaf gives a final answer, usually the most common outcome among the training examples that landed there. To predict for a new customer, you simply follow the questions from the top down to a leaf. A tree that grows too deep can memorize noise, so we often stop it early or trim it back.
