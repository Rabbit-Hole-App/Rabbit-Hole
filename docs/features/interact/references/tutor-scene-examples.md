# Tutor scene examples

Each example has a row for each depth. The **Story** is the full learner-facing narrative. **What the scene shows** turns its physical events into a visual experiment using components such as SVG ball glyphs, a launch mark, a release gate, arrows, and graphs.

## How to write the story and scene

### Story

Write the full text the learner will read. Begin with a situation, observation, or puzzle that gives them a reason to ask the question. Let the question develop through what happens next. Introduce a term when the learner needs it, and use a concrete example or a calculation where it helps them picture what is happening. Include the origin of an idea when it helps explain where the question or mechanism comes from.

Build toward an ending that resolves the opening question. A story can follow a person noticing something, a real experiment, or a problem unfolding in its own setting. Dialogue and fictional characters are optional. Keep the whole story in the table, including the setup, the discovery, and the ending.

### What the scene shows

Design a visual experiment from the story’s important events and relationships. Describe what appears first, what changes or gets compared, what the learner can observe, and which final view answers the question. Identify the visual components and what each represents. In the tennis example, the ball is an SVG glyph, the toss starts at a launch mark, and the opening hand becomes a release gate.

Explain the layout and the visual evidence in enough detail for someone to build the scene. Name the objects, paths, arrows, labels, guides, plots, and measurements that matter. State which marks share a scale, which quantities are compared, and what stays visible as the experiment advances. If two moments have the same position but different velocities, describe how the scene makes that distinction visible. Describe new components when the experiment needs them.

### Expectations by depth

| Depth | Story | What the scene shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Brief** | Start from a familiar situation and resolve one clear question. Introduce essential terms in plain language as the story needs them. Use a small example that helps the reader picture the result. | Build one clear comparison. Establish the starting state, show the important difference or change, and end with visible evidence for the answer. Use simple shapes and labels to make the relationship easy to read. |
| **Balanced** | Explain the mechanism through a worked example. Follow the important steps in order, explain what each quantity means, and use concrete values when they clarify the change. Return to the opening question with a causal explanation. | Make the mechanism observable. Specify the states or instants being compared, the scale, and the arrows or readouts that change. Keep the relevant invariant visible so the learner can distinguish what changes from what drives the change. |
| **Detailed** | Investigate why the result holds and under which assumptions. Derive or trace the governing relationship, work through its consequences, and resolve the original observation or an apparent contradiction. Use a contrasting case or boundary when it adds insight. | Make the reasoning inspectable. Choose measurements, diagrams, linked plots, or magnified views that expose the relationship. Specify units, reference points, and which quantities stay fixed. A controlled comparison can show what changes when an assumption or input changes. |

## How to define Mode and Controls

### Mode

The **Mode** column specifies how the experiment runs. Choose the mode from the scene’s timeline and the inputs the learner can change. Each depth can use any of the three modes.

| Mode | Timeline | Learner-adjustable scene parameters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Static** | None. The scene shows one or more still states. | Optional. Changing a parameter updates the still state. |
| **Over time** | Present. The scene follows a fixed sequence. | None. The experiment’s parameters are fixed. |
| **Interact** | Present. The scene follows a sequence that depends on the learner’s input. | At least one parameter changes how the experiment unfolds. |

Play, pause, replay, and scrubbing are timeline actions. For mode selection, count inputs that change the experiment itself, such as launch speed.

In the Mode cell, record the initial state, fixed values, assumptions, and quantities the learner can change. For a scene with a timeline, specify what time measures, its start and end, and the events at important instants. Describe what play, pause, replay, and scrubbing do, including which views update together. If a scene parameter changes, explain how the experiment recalculates and whether playback resumes or returns to a defined state.

### Controls

The **Controls** column maps the behavior in Mode to specific UI elements. Give each element’s visible label and type, such as a button, slider, selector, or timeline scrubber. Include the applicable units, default, minimum, maximum, step, or discrete options. For a scrubber, include its displayed range and marker labels. For a button that changes labels, map each label to its state.

For example, **Launch speed** maps to a slider in metres per second with a default of **5.0 m/s**, a range of **3.0–5.0 m/s**, and a step of **0.1 m/s**. The effect of changing launch speed belongs in **Mode**. Use **None** when a scene has no UI controls.

Every card has Reset (an icon at the top-right of the experiment). For scenes with a timeline, its parameters row sits below the transport (Play / Pause / Replay, scrubber, time); At top is a seek to the apex marker. For an adjustable still scene, the parameters row sits directly below the scene.

Append new examples below the same table header. Keep the same example number and user prompt across its depth rows. **Feedback** records the reviewer’s findings on the original scene. **Scene updated** contains the full revised experiment. **Review** records the scores, rank, and open checks.

## Reviewer system prompt

Use this prompt for future reviews. Give the reviewer the user prompt, assigned depth, full Story, What the scene shows, Mode, and Controls. Include a rendered scene or learner responses when they are available.

```text
You are an independent reviewer of Tutor scenes. Review each scene at its assigned depth. Complete Part 1 before Part 2. Base your findings on the supplied design, implementation, or learner responses. Identify which evidence you reviewed.

Part 1. Learning outcome and transfer

1. What is the end goal? State what the learner should be able to explain, predict, or do after the scene in one concrete sentence. Identify the starting question or misconception the scene needs to resolve. Fit the goal to Overview, Standard, or Deep.

2. What will the learner understand, and why? Trace the path from what they see or change to the conclusion they can draw. Point to the exact comparison, transition, or response to an input that supplies the evidence. Explain what a timeline or an adjustable parameter contributes when one is used. Identify any inference the learner would still have to guess.

3. Can the learner apply it? Give one new situation that requires the same idea at the assigned depth. Ask for a prediction, decision, or explanation. Give the expected answer and the reasoning that would show they transferred the idea.

4. Can the learner explain it to someone else? Give one teach-back prompt and state what a good answer would include. Look for an explanation of why the result happens in the learner's own words.

5. Could the scene teach it better? Identify the most important missing observation, explanation, or comparison. Consider whether a simpler view, a better example, or a meaningful interaction would improve understanding. Recommend the smallest useful change and say how it improves the learner's reasoning.

Report the likely outcome for a design review. Report demonstrated understanding only when learner responses provide evidence. State whether the described scene gives the learner enough evidence to answer both checks.

Part 2. Scene and UI quality

1. Does the scene's visual evidence answer the original question and support the learning goal from Part 1? Can the learner follow the initial state, what changes, and the resulting outcome?

2. Are the physics or other subject-matter claims correct under the stated assumptions? Check the timing, numerical values, units, directions, and relevant boundary cases. Flag a misleading comparison or a result the model does not support.

3. Can the learner read the evidence accurately? Check labels, units, shared scales, reference points, and time origins. Check that distinct quantities and overlapping traces are identifiable through labels or styles. Follow the visual order and check that the decisive comparison is easy to find.

4. Do the mode, timeline, and controls agree? Verify what is fixed, what the learner can change, what each control represents, and what updates together. Check relevant control labels, units, defaults, ranges, steps, markers, and states. Check what happens at start, pause, scrub, change of parameter, end, and replay when those actions apply.

5. Can a builder reproduce the scene? Check that the components, state changes, measurements, and final comparison are described clearly enough to build and verify. Flag missing implementation details and identify which checks require a rendered scene.

Return the review in two parts, in order. In Part 1, report the end goal, likely understanding, evidence in the scene, transfer check, teach-back check, and the highest-impact improvement. Include expected reasoning for both learner checks. In Part 2, report accuracy, visual clarity, mode and control consistency, and buildability. Separate changes needed for a correct explanation from optional polish.

Score Learning outcome and Scene and UI quality out of five each. Five meets the checks as written, four needs minor refinements, three needs a material repair, and one or two indicates major gaps. Explain each score with scene-specific evidence. A missing path to understanding or a materially incorrect experiment requires revision. For a batch, rank by total out of ten and use Learning outcome to resolve ties. Explain the reason for the order.
```

For the vertical-throw example, the learning goal is to explain why velocity can be zero while acceleration remains downward. A transfer check could ask what happens to a jumper’s vertical velocity and acceleration at the top of a jump in free flight. A teach-back answer should explain that gravity keeps changing vertical velocity through zero, which is the same reason the ball starts descending from its highest point.

### Questions used for the recorded review

- **Depth fit.** Does the scene teach the right idea at its assigned depth? Does the visual detail help answer the learner’s question?
- **Physics.** Are the motion, forces, measurements, and numerical results consistent with the stated model?
- **Visual evidence.** Can a new learner follow the starting state, the change, and the outcome? Can they see why the outcome answers the original question?
- **Comparisons.** Are the scales, time references, labels, and corresponding instants clear enough to compare the cases correctly?
- **Buildability.** Are the visual components and their states specified well enough to build? Do the scene, timeline behavior, and control definitions agree?

The first check informs Depth fit. The remaining checks inform the single Scene competence score.
