Why trees, and what a tree actually does

The setting. Your data is tabular: rows are samples, columns are features, and one column is the target you want to predict. The passage discusses why tree-based models are often a good fit for this kind of data.

Why trees do well here. The passage gives four reasons:

  • They handle mixed numeric and categorical features.
  • They capture nonlinear relationships through a sequence of decisions.
  • You can follow the path through a tree to see which decisions produced a prediction.
  • A random forest averages many trees to make predictions less sensitive to any one tree.

What one tree does. A decision tree partitions the feature space by asking one question at a time. Each node compares a feature against a threshold, for example Age < 30. Each leaf makes a prediction.

Classifying a sample. Start at the root and follow the branch matching the answer until you reach a leaf. In the loan example, a sample with Age < 30 goes left, then is asked whether Income > 50k. Yes leads to the leaf “Approve”; no to “Deny”. A sample with Age ≥ 30 follows a different branch.

Each path is a chain of conditions joined by “and”. For example, age under 30 and income over 50k describe a rectangular region when you plot age and income on separate axes.

How the tree gets built. At each node, choose the feature and threshold that best split the data under the chosen criterion. The passage's later sections discuss Gini impurity, entropy, and information gain.

Tracing one sample

Take a 28-year-old applicant earning 62k. Age is under 30, so follow the left branch. Income is above 50k, so follow the “yes” branch to “Approve”.

Only the questions on this path are used for that prediction.

Rectangular regions

Imagine age on the horizontal axis and income on the vertical axis. The age threshold draws a vertical line; the income threshold draws a horizontal one. The leaf covers the region that satisfies both conditions.

Why interpretability follows

For one prediction, you can list the decisions in the order they were applied. That sequence explains how the tree reached its leaf. It does not establish that changing a feature would cause a different real-world outcome.
