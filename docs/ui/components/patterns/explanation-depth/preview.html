Why trees, and what a tree actually does

A decision tree works like a flowchart for predictions. Each question checks one feature, like age. The answer picks a branch. The final stop, called a leaf, gives the prediction.

Different answers can send two people down different paths.

Why trees, and what a tree actually does

From root to leaf

Start at the root, the tree’s first question. Each question tests one feature and sends the case down a branch. Stop at a leaf to get the prediction. You can explain that prediction by tracing the tests along its path.

How the tree learns

During training, the tree tries questions such as “Age under 25?” and “Age under 30?” It chooses a split that groups examples with more similar outcomes, then repeats the process within those groups.

Approve or deny?

Age 25 · income $60k · credit score 640

  • Age under 30? Yes → check income. No → check credit score.
  • Income over $50k? Yes → Approve. No → Deny.

Prediction Approve. Both answers are yes, so this path reaches the Approve leaf without checking credit score.

Why trees, and what a tree actually does

One leaf, one region

The Approve path requires both age under 30 and income over $50k. Plot those two thresholds on separate axes and the intersection forms a rectangle.

Why a diagonal is harder

Each split is horizontal or vertical. A slanted boundary takes several cuts, forming a staircase.

How training picks a split

The tree chooses the question that most reduces impurity at this node. That local choice can lead to a different tree than optimizing every split together.

Which split wins?

Start with 4 approvals and 4 denials. Parent Gini is 0.5. Both splits make two groups of four.

Group counts are approvals / denials.
